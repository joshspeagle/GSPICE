Why can a covariance predict pixels? This page builds the answer from one spectrum to the
units that the code works in [@finkbeiner2025gspice].

## A spectrum as one draw from a Gaussian

A model must say which spectra are likely and which are not. Take many stars observed on
one wavelength grid. At pixel $i$, the flux differs from star to star, so pixel $i$ is
one variable, measured once in every spectrum. A spectrum of npix pixels is then one
point in npix dimensions.

GSPICE describes where these points lie with a
[multivariate Gaussian](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-multivariate-gaussian)
distribution. It has two parameters: a mean spectrum, and a
[covariance](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-covariance) $C$ between every pair of
pixels. [`gspice.fit()`](/GSPICE/reference/api/#gspice.fit) estimates both from the
[training spectra](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-training-spectra), and
[Fitting](/GSPICE/concepts/fitting/) explains how.
[What GSPICE needs](/GSPICE/start/fit/#what-gspice-needs) lists what this asks of your
spectra.

The spectra need not be Gaussian. A population of stars is far from a Gaussian
distribution. The conditional estimates of a Gaussian fitted to it still predict its
pixels well [@finkbeiner2025gspice].

## Covariance: lines vary together

One pixel can tell you about another only if the two vary together. Take two lines of
one element. A star with a deep line at pixel $i$ also tends to have a deep line at pixel
$i'$. Across the spectra, the fluxes at $i$ and $i'$ then rise and fall together.

The covariance $C_{ii'}$ measures this. It is positive where the two fluxes tend to lie
above their means together. It is negative where one tends to lie above its mean while
the other lies below, and 0 where they are unrelated. $C_{ii}$ is the variance at pixel $i$. The
[correlation](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-correlation)
$C_{ii'} / \sqrt{C_{ii} C_{i'i'}}$ puts this on a scale from −1 to 1.

## Conditioning on two pixels, then on many

A prediction answers this question: given the flux that you observed at some pixels, what
is the flux at the others? Start with two pixels, $i$ and $i'$, and measure each flux
from its mean, so that both means are 0. If you observe $x_{i'}$ at pixel $i'$, the flux
at pixel $i$ has a Gaussian distribution again [@rasmussen2006gpml]:

$$
x_i \mid x_{i'} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\frac{C_{ii'}}{C_{i'i'}}\, x_{i'},\;
C_{ii} - \frac{C_{ii'}^2}{C_{i'i'}}\right) .
$$

Its mean moves away from 0 in proportion to $x_{i'}$. Its variance is smaller than
$C_{ii}$, the more so the stronger the correlation.

The same holds for any number of pixels. Split the pixels into two sets. $G$ holds the
pixels to predict or to leave out, and $O$ holds the
[conditioning pixels](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-conditioning-pixels), whose values
the prediction uses. With $x$ measured from the mean again, the
[conditional distribution](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-conditional-distribution)
of $x_G$ is

$$
x_G \mid x_O \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(C_{GO} C_{OO}^{-1} x_O,\;
C_{GG} - C_{GO} C_{OO}^{-1} C_{OG}\right) .
$$

$C_{GO}$ is the block of $C$ with the rows $G$ and the columns $O$, and the other blocks
follow the same rule. The [conditional mean](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-conditional-mean)
is the prediction. It is the average of all spectra in the model that agree with the
conditioning pixels, and a linear function of $x_O$. Where $G$ holds all pixels, the
result is the [prior](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-prior) $\mathcal{N}(0, C)$.

A prediction can report only some pixels of $G$. The others are
[marginalized](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-marginalize): their values do not enter,
and the conditional covariance of the reported pixels includes the uncertainty of the
others. [Prediction](/GSPICE/concepts/prediction/) shows how each function chooses $G$.
The code computes the same distribution from the inverse of $C$, as
[The precision form](/GSPICE/concepts/numerics/#the-precision-form) explains.

## The uncertainty of a prediction

A prediction needs an uncertainty. The conditional covariance
$C_{GG} - C_{GO} C_{OO}^{-1} C_{OG}$ provides it. It depends on which pixels you
observed, but not on their values. Its diagonal holds the
[conditional variance](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-conditional-variance) of each
predicted pixel.

The training spectra carry their noise, so $C$ holds the noise as well as the variation
between the spectra. Every predicted variance therefore includes the noise of the
predicted pixel. The prediction describes the observed flux at the pixel, not its
noise-free flux.

Two checks compare the predicted uncertainty with the data:

- The [z-score](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-z-score) of one pixel is its flux minus
  the prediction, divided by the square root of the conditional variance.
- The [χ²](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-chi2) of several pixels uses their full
  conditional covariance ([χ² and its degrees of freedom](/GSPICE/concepts/prediction/#χ-and-its-degrees-of-freedom)).

Both have a known distribution only if the model is right. A model estimated from a
finite number of spectra predicts new spectra less well than it says
([Held-out uncertainty](/GSPICE/concepts/held-out/)).
[Diagnostics](/GSPICE/concepts/diagnostics/) measures both effects on your spectra.

## Working units

Spectra differ in brightness and in signal-to-noise ratio, and some pixels are bad. One
covariance of the raw flux would mix these differences with the variation that predicts
pixels. The model therefore describes the
[working vector](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-working-vector) $x_s$ of spectrum $s$,
not its flux. It computes $x_s$ in three steps:

<UnitsFigure />

1. Interpolate the flux linearly over the
   [bad pixels](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-bad-pixel), and hold it constant beyond
   the first and the last good pixel. This gives $\tilde f_s$. A bad pixel is never
   read, so its flux may be NaN.
2. Multiply by $a_s$, the square root of the mean of `ivar` over the good pixels of the
   spectrum, in flux⁻¹. This gives [scaled units](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-scaled-units).
3. Subtract the mean $m_b$ of the training spectra in the
   [S/N bin](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-S-N-bin) $b$ of the spectrum. There is one
   bin by default.

So

$$
x_s = a_s \tilde f_s - m_b .
$$

In scaled units, the noise at pixel $i$ has the variance $a_s^2 / \mathrm{ivar}_{si}$.
That variance is 1 where `ivar` is equal at every pixel of the spectrum, whatever its
S/N. A variance in scaled units divided by $a_s^2$ is a variance in flux units.
[Continuum normalization](/GSPICE/start/spectra/#continuum-normalization) says what the
scaling means for your spectra.

Two kinds of model use other working vectors:

| Model | Working vector | Units |
|---|---|---|
| Default, or with S/N bins | $x_s = a_s \tilde f_s - m_b$ | scaled |
| [Constant centre](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-constant-centre) $c$ | $x_s = a_s (\tilde f_s - c)$ | scaled |
| [Noise-aware](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-noise-aware-model) | $x_s = \tilde f_s - \mu$ | flux |

[Fitting](/GSPICE/concepts/fitting/) describes both. The public functions take flux and
return flux, flux² or unit-free values. The working units appear only in
[`gspice.scale()`](/GSPICE/reference/api/#gspice.scale),
[`gspice.unscale()`](/GSPICE/reference/api/#gspice.unscale) and the fields of
[`gspice.Model`](/GSPICE/reference/api/#gspice.Model).
