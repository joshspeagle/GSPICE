Why are z-scores wider on held-out spectra, and what does `inflate` do? In one sentence:
a model fitted to $N_s$ spectra has errors in its mean and covariance, and a new
spectrum sees all of them.

## Held-out and in-sample spectra

A [held-out spectrum](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-held-out-spectrum) is a spectrum
that the model was not fitted to, such as a spectrum that you repair. Its residuals
include the error of the estimated mean and covariance. A training spectrum is
different: it helped to fit the model, so the model leans toward it, and its
[in-sample](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-in-sample) residuals are smaller. The figure
shows both:

With few training spectra per pixel, the z-scores of held-out spectra are much wider
than 1. A threshold of 5 on such z-scores then flags far more pixels than a Gaussian
distribution would. With many training spectra per pixel, the effect becomes small.

## The finite-sample factor

The [finite-sample factor](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-finite-sample-factor) $f_s$
states the widening exactly. A model predicts $N_*$ pixels of a held-out spectrum from
$N_k$ [conditioning pixels](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-conditioning-pixels). Let
$Z^2$ be the [χ²](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-chi2) of those pixels, with their full
conditional covariance. Then $E[Z^2] / N_* = f_s$, with

$$
f_s = \left(1 + \frac{1}{N_s}\right) \frac{N_s - 2}{N_s - N_k - 2} \cdot
\frac{N_s - 1}{N_s - N_k - N_* - 2} .
$$

The factor grows as $N_s$ approaches $N_k$. It is not defined where
$N_s - N_k - N_* - 2 \le 0$. For one pixel of a pixelwise prediction, $N_* = 1$ and
$N_k$ is the number of pixels outside the guard window. The curve of the figure is the
square root of the mean of $f_s$ over the pixels, and it matches the points.
[What GSPICE needs](/GSPICE/start/fit/#what-gspice-needs) gives $f_s$ for a spectrum of
200 pixels, and [Fitting a model](/GSPICE/tutorials/fitting/#the-survey) for the survey
of the tutorials. [`gspice.finite_sample_factor()`](/GSPICE/reference/api/#gspice.finite_sample_factor)
computes $f_s$.

## Conditions

$f_s$ is exact under five conditions:

1. The model is dense and mean-centred: no low rank and no constant centre.
2. The model is unweighted and not noise-aware.
3. The working vectors are independent draws from one Gaussian distribution.
4. The pixel sets do not depend on the data.
5. $N_k$ counts the conditioning pixels only. Guard and ignored pixels do not count.

Condition 3 fails for scaled spectra with a spread of S/N, which are a mixture of
Gaussian distributions. Condition 4 fails for
[`gspice.repair()`](/GSPICE/reference/api/#gspice.repair), whose replaced and ignored
pixels depend on the z-scores. A model of another kind has no derived factor and
reports 1, although its held-out residuals are wider too.
[Calibration](/GSPICE/concepts/diagnostics/#calibration) measures the widening on your
own held-out spectra.

## inflate

`inflate=True` corrects the predicted uncertainty for this widening. `pixelwise`,
`infill` and `repair` then multiply each conditional variance and covariance by
$f_s(N_s, N_k, 1)$. Here $N_s$ counts the training spectra of the S/N bin of the
spectrum, and $N_k$ the conditioning pixels of the prediction. The deviation of each
[draw](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-draw) from the conditional mean grows by the
square root of that factor.

The factor of one pixel, $N_* = 1$, calibrates every fixed linear combination of the
predicted pixels, such as the flux in a band. The joint χ² of $N_*$ pixels needs
$f_s(N_s, N_k, N_*)$ instead, which is larger. `inflate=True` raises a `ValueError` for
a model without a derived factor, and where $f_s(N_s, N_k, 1)$ is not defined.
[Find outliers](/GSPICE/guides/outliers/#held-out-spectra-and-inflate) and
[Fill known bad pixels](/GSPICE/guides/infill/#the-variance-of-each-pixel) show the
effect on simulated spectra.

## Repaired.fs

`Repaired.fs` holds the factor that keeps `chi2 / (dof * fs)` at the mean 1 for
held-out Gaussian spectra, with or without `inflate`:

- without `inflate`, $f_s(N_s, N_k, \mathrm{dof})$;
- with `inflate=True`, $f_s(N_s, N_k, \mathrm{dof}) / f_s(N_s, N_k, 1)$, because
  `chi2` already uses the inflated covariance.

It is NaN where $f_s(N_s, N_k, \mathrm{dof})$ is not defined, and 1 for a model without
a derived factor.
