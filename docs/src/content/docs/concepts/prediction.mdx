How do [`gspice.pixelwise()`](/GSPICE/reference/api/#gspice.pixelwise) and
[`gspice.infill()`](/GSPICE/reference/api/#gspice.infill) choose which pixels to use?
Both use the [conditional distribution](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-conditional-distribution)
of [The model](/GSPICE/concepts/model/), and both return flux units. They differ in the
set $G$ of pixels that they do not condition on.

## Pixelwise prediction

To find [outliers](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-outlier), you need a prediction of
every pixel that does not use the pixel itself. `pixelwise` predicts each pixel $i$ from
the pixels beyond its [guard window](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-guard-window)
$g = \{i' : |i' - i| \le \mathrm{nguard}\}$, clipped at the ends of the spectrum:

<PixelSets kind="pixelwise" />

The [conditioning pixels](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-conditioning-pixels) $O$ are all
the pixels outside $g$. With $P$ the
[precision matrix](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-precision-matrix) of
[Numerics](/GSPICE/concepts/numerics/#the-precision-form),

$$
x_i \mid x_O \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(-\big(P_{gg}^{-1} P_{gO}\, x_O\big)_i,\;
\big(P_{gg}^{-1}\big)_{ii}\right) .
$$

The other pixels of $g$ are [marginalized](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-marginalize).

### The guard window

The guard window keeps the neighbours of pixel $i$ out of its prediction, for two
reasons. First, neighbouring pixels are correlated through the line-spread function and
the upstream processing. The prediction should come from the correlations of the
spectra instead [@finkbeiner2025gspice]. Second, an artifact a few pixels wide also
changes the neighbours of $i$, and a prediction from them would follow the artifact.

The default `nguard=20` is the paper's choice, set by the size of the smoothing kernel
of its continuum normalization. `nguard >= npix - 1` gives the
[prior](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-prior). [The guard window](/GSPICE/guides/outliers/#the-guard-window)
measures the effect of `nguard` on simulated artifacts.

### Bad pixels and the z-score

`pixelwise` conditions on the [bad pixels](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-bad-pixel)
through their interpolated values. Their z-score is NaN, and the variance omits the
error of the interpolated values. Next to a long run of bad pixels, the z-scores of the
good pixels are therefore not calibrated. An interpolated bad pixel beyond the guard
window can also carry the flux of a good pixel inside it into the prediction.

The [z-score](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-z-score) of pixel $i$ of spectrum $s$ is

$$
z_{si} = \frac{f_{si} - \hat f_{si}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{var}_{si}}} ,
$$

with $\hat f_{si}$ and $\mathrm{var}_{si}$ the conditional mean and variance in flux
units. [`gspice.Prediction`](/GSPICE/reference/api/#gspice.Prediction) holds the three
arrays.

## Infill

To replace pixels, you predict them from all the pixels that you trust. `infill`
divides the pixels of each spectrum into four kinds:

<PixelSets kind="infill" />

- the [replaced pixels](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-replaced-pixel) $M$, given by
  `replace`, the bad pixels by default;
- the [ignored pixels](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-ignored-pixel), given by
  `ignore`, which are neither conditioned on nor replaced, such as a guard window around
  a range of pixels;
- the bad pixels, which are never conditioned on;
- the conditioning pixels $O$, all the other pixels.

$G$ is the union of `replace`, `ignore` and the bad pixels:

$$
x_G \mid x_O \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(-P_{GG}^{-1} P_{GO}\, x_O,\; P_{GG}^{-1}\right) .
$$

A pixel in both `replace` and `ignore` is replaced. The pixels of $G$ outside $M$ keep
their input values and are marginalized. Unlike `pixelwise`, `infill` never conditions
on a bad pixel, and no value in $G$ enters the mean, so an outlier in $G$ cannot change
it.

A [draw](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-draw) is a random sample of $x_M$ from this
distribution. [Fill known bad pixels](/GSPICE/guides/infill/) shows the mean, the
variance, the covariance and the draws, and when to use each.
[What you get back](/GSPICE/start/results/#repaired) lists the fields of the result.

## χ² and its degrees of freedom

A prediction of several pixels needs one number that says whether the observed values
agree with it. `Repaired.chi2` compares the input values with their prediction at $T$,
the replaced pixels that are good in the input:

$$
\chi^2 = r_T^\mathsf{T} C_{TT}^{-1} r_T ,
$$

with $r_T$ the input values minus the conditional mean, and $C_{TT}$ their conditional
covariance. A bad pixel has no input value to compare, so it is in $M$ but not in $T$.
`Repaired.dof`, the number of degrees of freedom, is $|T|$.

The [χ²](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-chi2) uses the full conditional covariance, so
the correlated errors of neighbouring pixels do not count twice. Suppose that the mean and
the covariance were exact, and that the pixel sets did not depend on the data. Then
$\chi^2$ would follow the χ² distribution with $|T|$ degrees of freedom, whose mean is
$|T|$. A fitted model predicts a
[held-out spectrum](/GSPICE/concepts/glossary/#term-held-out-spectrum) less well:
the mean of $\chi^2 / |T|$ is then the finite-sample factor of
[Held-out uncertainty](/GSPICE/concepts/held-out/), which `Repaired.fs` holds.
