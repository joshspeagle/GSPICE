GSPICE suits a large set of similar spectra on one wavelength grid, in which some pixels
are bad. This page lists what it needs, what it fixes and where it fails.

## What GSPICE needs

GSPICE learns from your spectra how the flux at each pixel varies with the flux at every
other pixel. It treats each pixel as one variable, measured once in every spectrum:

<PixelGrid />

So the spectra must agree pixel by pixel:

- **One wavelength grid.** Pixel $i$ has the same wavelength in every spectrum, for
  example after a shift to the rest frame. [Prepare your spectra](/GSPICE/start/spectra/)
  explains the choice of frame.
- **A similar line-spread function.** A line of the same depth must cover the same
  pixels in every spectrum.
- **Many training spectra.** [`gspice.fit()`](/GSPICE/reference/api/#gspice.fit)
  estimates a covariance between every pair of pixels from the
  [training spectra](/GSPICE/background/glossary/#term-training-spectra).

The default model needs at least npix + 1 training spectra, for npix pixels. By default,
`fit` uses a training spectrum only if it has at most 64 bad pixels. It raises a
`ValueError` if too few spectra remain.

At that minimum the covariance is noisy. The uncertainty of a prediction for a held-out
spectrum is then too small. This code shows by how much, for a spectrum of 200 pixels:

```python
import gspice

npix = 200
for n_train in (300, 1000, 3000, 10000):
    factor = gspice.finite_sample_factor(n_train, npix - 1)
    print(f"{n_train:>6} training spectra: {factor:.2f}")
```

```text output
   300 training spectra: 9.21
  1000 training spectra: 1.57
  3000 training spectra: 1.15
 10000 training spectra: 1.04
```

Each factor is the mean squared error of a prediction divided by its variance from
GSPICE. It holds for one pixel of a
[held-out spectrum](/GSPICE/background/glossary/#term-held-out-spectrum), predicted from
the other 199, if the spectra are Gaussian. With 300 training spectra, the squared
errors are 9.21 times the variance on average. With 10000, they are 1.04 times the
variance. [The finite-sample factor](/GSPICE/background/prediction/#the-finite-sample-factor)
explains this factor, and the option `inflate=True` that corrects for it.

If you have fewer spectra than pixels, fit a
[low-rank model](/GSPICE/background/glossary/#term-low-rank-model) with `fit(..., rank=K)`.
It needs only $K + 2$ training spectra. [Choose a model](/GSPICE/guides/models/#low-rank)
shows one.

## What GSPICE fixes

- **Bad pixels that you know.** A bad pixel has a nonzero `mask` or `ivar == 0`.
  [`gspice.infill()`](/GSPICE/reference/api/#gspice.infill) replaces it by its
  prediction from the other pixels, with a variance.
- **Artifacts that you do not know.** An artifact is a corruption of the data, such as a
  cosmic ray or a hot column. [`gspice.repair()`](/GSPICE/reference/api/#gspice.repair)
  finds the pixels far from their prediction, and replaces them.

## Where GSPICE fails

- **Long shifted segments.** A segment with a shifted wavelength scale can cover hundreds
  of pixels. Inside it, the pixels that predict each pixel are shifted too, so `repair`
  misses much of it. [Long defects](/GSPICE/background/repair/#long-defects) measures
  this limit.
- **Artifacts at the same pixels in every training spectrum.** `fit` learns them as part
  of the spectra, so `repair` does not find them.

## GSPICE and interpolation

Linear interpolation across a gap draws a straight line between the two pixels at its
ends. It cannot restore an absorption line inside the gap, and it gives no uncertainty.
GSPICE predicts the gap from all the pixels outside it, through the correlations of the
training spectra. It also gives the variance of each predicted pixel, and the covariance
between them.
