Are the uncertainties right, and is my `ivar` right? Two functions answer on
[held-out spectra](/GSPICE/background/glossary/#term-held-out-spectrum), which the model
was not fitted to. [Diagnostics](/GSPICE/background/diagnostics/) gives their equations.

## Set up

The model is fitted to 4000 simulated spectra of 100 pixels. The checks use 1000 other
[clean spectra](/GSPICE/background/glossary/#term-clean-spectrum), without artifacts.

```python
import numpy as np

import gspice

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
train = gspice.simulate(4000, 100, rng=rng)
held = gspice.simulate(1000, 100, rng=rng)
model, _ = gspice.fit(train.flux, train.ivar)
```

## Check the uncertainties

A prediction comes with a variance, and you need to know whether it is right.
[`gspice.calibration()`](/GSPICE/reference/api/#gspice.calibration) hides pixels of each
spectrum at random, predicts them, and compares:

<CalibrationSteps />

```python
result = gspice.calibration(model, held.flux, held.ivar, rng=rng)
mean, sem = result.ratio()
print("n:", result.n[0])
print("mean:", mean.round(2))
print("standard error:", sem.round(2))
print("tail:", result.tail(0.99).round(3))
```

```text output
n: [ 1  4 16]
mean: [0.99 0.92 0.97]
standard error: [0.05 0.02 0.01]
tail: [0.012 0.005 0.01 ]
```

### How to read the numbers

Each column is one number $n$ of hidden pixels, a run of consecutive good pixels.

- **mean** is the mean of $z^2 / (n f_s)$ over the spectra, with $f_s$ the
  [finite-sample factor](/GSPICE/background/glossary/#term-finite-sample-factor). It is
  1 for a correct model. Read it with its **standard error**: a mean within about two
  standard errors of 1 agrees with 1.
- **tail** is the fraction of spectra with $z^2 / f_s$ above the 99% quantile of the χ²
  distribution with $n$ degrees of freedom. It is near 0.01 for Gaussian spectra.

Here the means for $n = 4$ and $n = 16$ are 0.92 and 0.97, four and three standard
errors below 1: the predicted variances of these runs are 3% to 8% too large. The
simulated spectra are not Gaussian, and
[Calibration](/GSPICE/background/diagnostics/#calibration) explains how that can move
the mean.

### When the mean is off

A mean above 1 shows predicted variances that are too small. A mean below 1 shows
variances that are too large. Artifacts in the checked spectra are a common cause:

```python
dirty = gspice.simulate(1000, 100, rng=rng, artifacts=0.3)
mean, _ = gspice.calibration(model, dirty.flux, dirty.ivar, rng=rng).ratio()
print("mean:", mean.round(1))
```

```text output
mean: [89.  50.3 82.8]
```

Here 30% of the spectra have an artifact, and the means are far above 1. Check the
causes in this order:

| Cause | Sign | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Artifacts in the checked spectra | above 1 | Check on clean spectra, or mark the artifacts in `mask` |
| The checked spectra are training spectra | below 1 | Check on held-out spectra |
| Spectra of very different S/N share one covariance | either, by S/N | Compare the means by S/N with `result.ratio(where=...)`; fit with `snr_bins` |
| Artifacts in the training spectra widen the covariance | below 1 | Remove the spectra with artifacts from the training set |
| A mean that changes with $n$ | either | The covariance describes the correlations less well than the variances |

## Check the noise

Every model trusts `ivar`, the inverse variance of the noise. A model without
`noise=True` takes into its covariance a noise level that all its training spectra
share. Its z-scores then cannot show an `ivar` that is wrong in all of them.
[`gspice.noise_audit()`](/GSPICE/reference/api/#gspice.noise_audit) can:

```python
rho = gspice.noise_audit(model, held.flux, held.ivar)
print(f"median {np.median(rho):.3f}, smallest {rho.min():.3f}")
```

```text output
median 1.051, smallest 0.976
```

### How to read the numbers

`rho` holds $\rho$ at each pixel: the root mean of the squared residual of the
pixelwise prediction, times `ivar`, over the spectra. Where `ivar` is right, $\rho^2$ is
at least 1 in expectation. The error of the prediction adds to it, so $\rho$ is a little
above 1 here.

$\rho^2$ of one pixel scatters by about $\sqrt{2/m}$ for $m$ spectra, 0.045 for these
1000. So a pixel slightly below 1 can occur by chance.

### When rho is off

A $\rho$ well below 1 shows that `ivar` reports more noise than the spectra have. In
this example, `ivar` reports twice the variance at pixels 0 to 49:

```python
wrong = np.array(held.ivar)
wrong[:, :50] /= 2
rho = gspice.noise_audit(model, held.flux, wrong)
print(f"pixels 0 to 49: {np.median(rho[:50]):.2f}")
print(f"pixels 50 to 99: {np.median(rho[50:]):.2f}")
```

```text output
pixels 0 to 49: 0.75
pixels 50 to 99: 1.04
```

Find out how your pipeline computes `ivar` at the pixels with a low $\rho$. A $\rho$
well above 1 can come from noise that `ivar` omits or from a poor prediction, so compare
it with the calibration above. Run `noise_audit` only on held-out spectra: on the
training spectra, the residuals are too small.
